\olsection{Necessity of Existence}

Combining standard first-order logic with identity and modal logic 
allows us to prove that everything exists necessarily. 

\begin{defn}[Necessity of Existence] The necessity of existence is
    the claim that everything exists necessarily. For a particular
    !!{constant} $\Obj{a}$, it is the claim that necessarily, $\Obj{a}$ is something,
    i.e., that $\Obj{a}$ exists:
    $$\Box\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$$
    Or equivalently, using the existence predicate $\lfrexists$:
    $$\Box\lfrexists \Obj{a}$$
    We can also express it as a universal claim:
    $$\lforall[x][\lexists[y][\eq[x][y]]]$$
    $$\lforall[x][\lfrexists x]$$
\end{defn}

These claims are highly controversial, of course. However, they follow
from core principles of first-order logic and modal logic. 

The proof of necessity of existence relies on two claims:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item It is necessary that $\Obj{a}$ is $\Obj{a}$. 
    \item It is necessary that if $\Obj{a}$ is $\Obj{a}$, then $\Obj{a}$ exists. 
\end{enumerate}
Together with the \emph{Distribution} principle of modal logic:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Distriubtion} (Necessity is Closed under Necessary Consequence).
If it is necessary that if $\Obj \PVarP$ then $\Obj \PVarQ$, then if 
it is necessary that $\Obj \PVarP$, it is necessary that $\Obj \PVarQ$.\\
$\Box(\Obj \PVarP\lif \Obj \PVarQ)\lif (\Box\Obj \PVarP\lif\Box\Obj \PVarQ)$.
\footnote{This principle is called `distribution' because it `distributes'
the $\Box$ operator over the conditional~$\lif$.}
\end{itemize}
The two claims together with the principle entail that necessarily,
$\Obj{a}$ exists. 

The two claims can be derived from Reflexivity, Classical Existential
Implication and the Necessitation rule: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reflexivity. For any name $\Obj{a}$, `$\Obj{a}$ is $\Obj{a}$' is a logical truth.
    \item Classical Existential Implication. If something is true of $\Obj{a}$, then $\Obj{a}$ exists.
    \item Necessitation rule. If $!A$ is a logical truth, $\Box!A$ is
    a logical truth.
\end{itemize}
The first claim is derived from Reflexivity and the Necessitation
rule. The second claim is derived from Classical Existential
Implication and the Necessitation rule. 

In natural deduction, Reflexivity corresponds to \Intro{\eq}
in first-order logic and positive free logic, 
Classical Existential Implication is an application of the 
\Intro{\lexists} rule of first-order logic, and Distribution and
 the Necessitation rule are both captured by $\Box \Ax{K}$.

The proof can be put in natural deduction as follows:

\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{}
    \RightLabel{\Intro{\eq}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\eq[\Obj{a}][\Obj{a}]$}
    \RightLabel{\Intro{\lexists}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \RightLabel{$\Box$\Ax{K}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\Box\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
\end{prooftree}

For generalized Necessity of Existence, we add a \Intro{\lforall}
step. 
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{}
    \RightLabel{\Intro{\eq}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\eq[\Obj{a}][\Obj{a}]$}
    \RightLabel{\Intro{\lexists}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\lexists[y][\eq[\Obj{a}][y]]$}
    \RightLabel{$\Box$\Ax{K}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\Box\lexists[y][\eq[\Obj{a}][y]]$}
    \RightLabel{\Intro{\lforall}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\lforall[x][\Box\lexists[y][\eq[x][y]]]$}
\end{prooftree}
Note that the restrictions on \Intro{\lforall} are satisfied since 
$\Obj{a}$ doesn't appear in assumptions that are undischarged at the time
of that step nor in the conclusion of the step.

The conclusion, that everything necessarily exist, is highly 
controversial. Since, however, it follows from Reflexivity, 
Necessitation and Distribution, and Classical Existential 
Implication, anyone who rejects it must reject one of these 
principles. 

Free logics reject Classical Existential Implications. In those 
logics, the $\Intro{\lexists}$ rule is rejected. We instead have 
the weaker free quantification rule:
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{$\lfrexists\Obj{a}$}
        \AxiomC{$!A[a/x]$}
    \RightLabel{\Intro{\lexists}$_{\Log{FL}}$}
    \BinaryInfC{$!A$}
\end{prooftree}

This blocks the original proof of Necessary of Existence. To use the 
weaker \Intro{\lexists}$_{\Log{FL}}$ rule, we would to assume that 
$\Obj{a}$ exists, but the only conclusions we then get are trivial. 
Here's one failed attempt at proving the Necessity of Existence in 
free quantified modal logic:
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{$\lfrexists\Obj{a}$}
        \AxiomC{}
        \RightLabel{\Intro{\eq}}
        \UnaryInfC{$\eq[\Obj{a}][\Obj{a}]$}
    \RightLabel{\Intro{\lexists}$_{\Log{FL}}$}
    \BinaryInfC{$\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \RightLabel{{\color{red}$\Box$\Ax{K}}\quad \textbf{Incorrect}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\Box\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
\end{prooftree}
The $\Box\Ax{K}$ step is incorrect here. We're trying to get $\Box!A$
from !!a{derivation} of $!A$, but that's only possible if the
!!a{derivation} has no undischarged assumptions, or if we have the
necessitated version of each of its premises. Here the derivation has
$\lfrexists\Obj{a}$ as undischarged assumption, so the $\Box\Ax{K}$ step
is incorrect.

Both ways of `fixing' the !!{derivation} above for free logic lead 
to correct !!{derivation}s whose conclusion is not the Necessity
of Existence but a trivial claim. 

First, we may try and discharge the $\lfrexists\Obj{a}$ before
 $\Box\Ax{K}$:
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{$\Discharge{\lfrexists\Obj{a}}{1}$}
        \AxiomC{}
        \RightLabel{\Intro{\eq}}
        \UnaryInfC{$\eq[\Obj{a}][\Obj{a}]$}
    \RightLabel{\Intro{\lexists}$_{\Log{FL}}$}
    \BinaryInfC{$\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \DischargeRule{\Intro{\lif}}{1}
    \UnaryInfC{$\lfrexists\Obj{a} \lif \lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \RightLabel{$\Box$\Ax{K}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\Box(\lfrexists\Obj{a} \lif \lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]])$}
\end{prooftree}
This !!{derivation} is correct in \emph{positive} free quantified modal logic. 
In the negative variant, 
\Intro{\eq} is rejected, for we can only infer $\eq[\Obj{a}][\Obj{a}]$ if we 
have the assumption that $\Obj{a}$ exists. As it happens, there's no 
problem with making that assumption here, since we're making it 
for the \Intro{\lexists}$_{\Log{FL}}$ step anyway. So,
we can also get !!a{derivation} for negative free quantified modal logic:
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{$\Discharge{\lfrexists\Obj{a}}{1}$}
        \AxiomC{$\Discharge{\lfrexists\Obj{a}}{1}$}
        \RightLabel{\Intro{\eq}$_{\Log{NFL}}$}
        \UnaryInfC{$\eq[\Obj{a}][\Obj{a}]$}
    \RightLabel{\Intro{\lexists}$_{\Log{FL}}$}
    \BinaryInfC{$\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \DischargeRule{\Intro{\lif}}{1}
    \UnaryInfC{$\lfrexists\Obj{a} \lif \lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \RightLabel{$\Box$\Ax{K}}
    \UnaryInfC{$\Box(\lfrexists\Obj{a} \lif \lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]])$}
\end{prooftree}
Either way, the conclusion is trivial. It simply says that
necessarily, $\Obj{a}$ exists if $\Obj{a}$ exists. In some versions of the
language of free logic, $\lfrexists\Obj{a}$ merely \emph{abbreviates}
$\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$; in those versions the conclusion is just
$\Box(\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]] \lif \lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]])$.

Alternatively, we may supplement the mistaken $\Box\Ax{K}$ step 
with the assumption that $\Box \lfrexists\Obj{a}$:
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{$\Discharge{\Box\lfrexists\Obj{a}}{2}$}
        \AxiomC{$\Discharge{\lfrexists\Obj{a}}{1}$}
            \AxiomC{}
            \RightLabel{\Intro{\eq}}
            \UnaryInfC{$\eq[\Obj{a}][\Obj{a}]$}
        \RightLabel{\Intro{\lexists}$_{\Log{FL}}$}
        \BinaryInfC{$\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \DischargeRule{$\Box$\Ax{K}}{1}
    \BinaryInfC{$\Box\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \DischargeRule{\Intro{\lif}}{2}
    \UnaryInfC{$\Box\lfrexists\Obj{a}\lif \Box\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
\end{prooftree}
This !!{derivation} is correct in \emph{positive} free quantified
modal logic. As before, there is a variant suitable for the negative
variant instead:
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{$\Discharge{\Box\lfrexists\Obj{a}}{2}$}
        \AxiomC{$\Discharge{\lfrexists\Obj{a}}{1}$}
            \AxiomC{$\Discharge{\lfrexists\Obj{a}}{1}$}
            \RightLabel{\Intro{\eq}$_{\Log{NFL}}$}
            \UnaryInfC{$\eq[\Obj{a}][\Obj{a}]$}
        \RightLabel{\Intro{\lexists}$_{\Log{FL}}$}
        \BinaryInfC{$\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \DischargeRule{$\Box$\Ax{K}}{1}
    \BinaryInfC{$\Box\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
    \DischargeRule{\Intro{\lif}}{2}
    \UnaryInfC{$\Box\lfrexists\Obj{a}\lif \Box\lexists[x][\eq[\Obj{a}][x]]$}
\end{prooftree}
Either way, the conclusion is also trivial. For it states that:
if $\Obj{a}$ necessarily exists, then $\Obj{a}$ is 
necessarily something. 

In terms of models, the Necessity of Existence holds in 
fixed-domain models. To avoid it, we need a \emph{variable}
domains: the domain of individuals may change from world to world. 
This means that a constant like $\Obj{a}$ may denote an object that is not
in the domain of some worlds. We must then evaluate formulas like 
$\Obj{Fa}$ or $\eq[\Obj{a}][\Obj{b}]$ at worlds \emph{where $\Obj{a}$ doesn't denote
anything in the domain}. Classical semantics for predicate logic 
doesn't have a way to do that. Free logics have been designed to 
accommodate denotation-less constants. Hence a Quantified Modal Logic
that avoids the necessity of existence must combine modal logic with 
\emph{free} first order logic logic.
